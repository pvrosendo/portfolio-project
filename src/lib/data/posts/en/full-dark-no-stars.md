After finishing **It**, I genuinely believed I’d struggle to find another book quite as visceral. But King caught me off guard once again. The story of Pennywise functioned almost like a massive adventure blending everything: horror, drama, suspense, and cosmic fantasy. In a melancholic, touching way, it chronicled the journey of a group of childhood friends overcoming their deepest traumas.

> *Here, the game was entirely different.*

The collection brings together four novellas (plus a razor-sharp afterword) tackling wildly distinct premises. You’ll find intense suspense, psychological dread, and raw drama, but virtually zero fantasy. The atmosphere throughout these pages is stark: there's no spirit of adventure. It’s simply bizarre, disturbing, and designed to drag the reader face-to-face with the darkest corners of human nature.

---

## 1922

The opening novella hits like an immediate punch to the gut. The story starts right off the bat with a written murder confession, but you instantly realize this is no ordinary case: a farmer admits to brutally slaughtering his own wife with the assistance of their 14-year-old son, dumping her body down an abandoned well.

My immediate reaction was a mix of: *"Wait, you're handing me the final punchline on a silver platter right on page one?"*. But the reality sank in fast, and the intended hook worked like a charm: I was instantly obsessed with understanding what kind of demons could drive a father to commit such an atrocity HAND IN HAND with his own teenage kid.

With that killer hook, the narrative unfolds and the motives become crystal clear. The wife, Arlette, inherits a massive plot of farmland and decides to sell it off to an industrial livestock corporation, eager to cash out and start fresh in Omaha. But her husband, Wilfred, sees things entirely differently: he is viscerally tethered to the soil, sees immense potential in farming, and has zero interest in surrendering to city life.

The story lays bare how pride, bitterness, and greed can corrode even the closest bonds. Wilfred, already watching his marriage crumble, stops viewing his wife as a partner and starts treating her as a direct rival in a bitter land dispute.

Realizing he can't sway Arlette through reason, Wilf begins coldly manipulating their 14-year-old son, Henry (Hank). He grooms the boy into believing his mother is the true villain and that moving to the city would destroy them. The manipulation spirals into sickness as Wilf convinces the kid that "getting rid" of his mother is their only ticket to peace.

> *What's fascinating here is how King illustrates just how easily a teenager's mind can be warped and weaponized — something he does masterfully, just like in It. Reading through it, you realize how monstrous it is for a son to help butcher his own mother, yet for a 14-year-old kid under that pressure, it steadily started making twisted sense.*

> *And it's even wilder knowing that real-life cases like this have actually happened. It proves that the human mind can venture into terrifying territory when moral compasses shatter.*

The murder itself is grotesque and visceral. While Arlette sleeps, they throw a burlap sack over her head and butcher her with a meat cleaver. Pure nightmare fuel.

> *The way this scene is paced demonstrates exactly why I appreciate King’s deliberate, detailed prose. His slow burn strikes the perfect balance between atmospheric depth and narrative momentum — unlike the drag I felt reading Tolkien's Fellowship of the Ring; but that's a story for another post.*

Once the deed is done, Wilf dumps her body into an abandoned well on the farm. They craft a cover story claiming Arlette ran off in the dead of night, assuming no one would ever suspect. But the local sheriff begins snooping around — pressured by the agribusiness firm eager to buy the acres. With the heat turning up, Wilf goes a step further into madness: he slaughters Elpis, one of the farm’s cows, and tosses the carcass down the well on top of his wife's corpse to mask the smell.

That’s where the novella hits its stride, exposing how fragile the mind becomes under the weight of guilt. A grotesque swarm of rats overruns the farm, lured by the rotting flesh at the bottom of the well. Wilf is traumatized when he spots the rodents chewing on Arlette's remains, and in his panic, takes a nasty rat bite to the hand. The wound turns gangrenous and he is forced to have it amputated. It’s a textbook recipe for disaster: a broken mind, a mutilated body, and sanity circling the drain — ***and not just his***.

Meanwhile, Hank’s psychological state unravels even faster. Shouldering the crushing guilt of helping butcher his own mother, while maintaining lies to the entire town, proves an impossible burden for a 14-year-old.

Hank’s total downfall is sealed when he gets his high-school sweetheart, Shannon, pregnant. Her wealthy, furious parents ship her off to a strict Catholic home to keep her away from Hank. It fails: the teens break out together and take to the highway as armed robbers, earning national notoriety as "The Sweetheart Bandits". Tragedy is inevitable. A pregnant Shannon is shot dead in a blizzard shootout; Hank, shattered by grief and mental torment, shoots himself in the head.

When Wilfred travels to the morgue to identify the bodies, horror reaches its zenith: the faces of his son and daughter-in-law have been partially gnawed on by rats — his waking nightmare made flesh.

Wilf loses his mind completely. Forced to sell off the very land he committed atrocities to keep, he drifts into the city he always despised, scraping by on odd jobs. An old, crippled man hollowed out by paranoia. His end is suffocatingly bleak: he commits suicide in a dingy hotel room, surrounded by hallucinations of the rats that never stopped stalking him since the day he killed Arlette.

> *This novella is brilliant, and Netflix put out a great adaptation under the same title: 1922. Highly worth checking out. And if you think reading this summary ruins the experience, think again. The journey is well worth it regardless.*

## Big Driver

This novella might easily be the most visceral piece of writing I’ve read from King so far. I was genuinely stunned by how gripping and suffocating the story was.

The protagonist is Tess, a mystery novelist in her late 30s or 40s who makes a quiet living writing cozy, lighthearted whodunits starring an elderly sleuth named Doreen. To supplement her book royalties, she takes occasional speaking gigs for local women's book clubs. Her personal golden rule for these trips is rigid: she always drives her own car and stays away at most one night, so she doesn't leave her cat alone for too long.

Tess accepts a last-minute invitation to speak at a library in Chicopee, organized by a jolly, heavy-set woman named Ramona Norville. The event is a smash hit, packing around 400 ladies into the audience.

Afterward over coffee, Ramona casually suggests a shortcut so the writer can bypass Friday rush-hour traffic on the turnpike. Tess happily takes the advice and punches the route into her GPS — which she affectionately calls "Tom" — heading down Stagg Road: a forgotten rural backroad hemmed in by overgrown brush and completely desolate.

Following the backroad, she suddenly hits wooden planks studded with rusted spikes scattered across the pavement. Her front tire instantly blows out. An old van belonging to a band called the Zombie Confectioners swerves around the debris and speeds off without stopping.

With no cell signal, Tess pulls into the gravel lot of an abandoned gas station and grocery store, an old soda sign creaking in the wind. Cursing her bad luck, relief washes over her when she spots a truck pulling up. *"What luck"*.

Out of a blue Ford F-150 steps an absolute giant — over 6-foot-6 and easily 300 pounds —, wearing filthy overalls, a stained trucker cap, and a red-stone ring on his pinky. He strolls over pretending to be helpful with the flat tire. The thing is, glancing into the bed of his pickup, Tess spots more of the exact same spike-studded boards. She realizes it's an ambush, but before she can react, a crushing right hook knocks her cold to the gravel.

> *This was one of those classic "expected surprises". Given the setup, alarm bells were ringing from the second she turned onto that road. When the spiked boards showed up, I knew things were about to go south fast (I just didn't expect it to go this dark).*

> *Even though I anticipated bad news, I wasn't mentally braced for what came next. Initially, I expected some over-the-top gore or something supernatural. That's probably why it hit me so hard: what followed was the most sickeningly human, grounded cruelty imaginable.*

The giant drags the unconscious writer into the filthy backroom of the abandoned store. What follows is sustained torment: she is brutally beaten, repeatedly raped, and choked within an inch of her life. In fleeting moments of semiconsciousness, Tess watches her attacker jumping and dancing around her, loudly belting out the Rolling Stones' *Brown Sugar*. Before leaving, he tears off her underwear and pockets it as a trophy.

> *I always like reading with a soundtrack playing in the background (soundtracks from games like Dark Souls, The Witcher, Life is Strange, ambient or dark fantasy) to set the mood and heighten immersion. I was listening to my standard horror dark fantasy playlist, but the second that scene started, I queued up Brown Sugar on Spotify, and the vibe was utterly surreal. Easily one of the most intense reading experiences of my life. It felt like watching the nightmare unfold in real time.*

> *It’s incredible how King can make a scene feel so unhinged without losing narrative control. Placing a 6'6", 300-pound monster singing and dancing after committing unspeakable acts is chilling. It made me pause and reflect on the darkest corridors of human psychology: What would a therapist say about this? Is it pure trauma, twisted upbringing, or raw psychopathy? Fiction that genuinely prompts those questions is something I really appreciate.*

Assuming he strangled her to death, the man carries her body along the shoulder, plants a cynical kiss on her cheek, and shoves her down into a drainage culvert running underneath the road, buried under rotting leaves and freezing sludge.

Once the truck rumbles away, Tess regains consciousness in the pitch-black pipe. Crawling toward the opening, her hand brushes against something soft: two decomposing female corpses. *She was far from the first victim.*

Fueled by sheer terror and adrenaline, Tess drags herself out of that watery grave. She creeps back into the store to gather what remains of her clothes, finding her earrings stolen and her phone smashed to pieces. Wrapping a scrap of filthy carpet over her shoulders, she begins a harrowing trek down Stagg Road in the dead of night, diving into the brush every time headlights sweep past.

> *I vividly remember this sequence. She falls into a desperate mental loop of "Why?", and I found myself asking the exact same questions, feeling pure, heavy empathy for her.*

Slipping past a noisy dive bar where the Zombie Confectioners are playing, Tess reaches a brightly lit highway gas station. At a beat-up payphone, she scrapes grime off the metal to jot down notes and calls a town-car service she frequently relies on. The driver picks her up without asking invasive questions about her bloodied state.

Back home, Tess scrubs the dried blood from her skin, deadbolts every lock, and feeds her cat. The dread of public exposure paralyzes her: terrified of becoming tabloid fodder and having her personal trauma dissected by the media, she adamantly refuses to go to a hospital or contact the police. She even hammers her own staircase with a meat mallet to fake bruises, telling her neighbor she took a tumble over the cat.

> *Heartbreaking to think about how devastatingly sad and real that fear of public scrutiny is.*

Trauma fractures her psyche, sparking intense debates with mental projections: the rational voice of Tom (the GPS) and the sharp deductions of Doreen (her fictional elderly detective). Those voices push her: the women in that pipe deserve justice.

> *What amazed me most was how she didn't completely shut down. Instead of collapsing, she channeled that trauma into ice-cold resolve. Wild.*

Determined to settle the score herself, Tess grabs a .38 caliber Smith & Wesson revolver from her closet, rents an inconspicuous car, and heads back out into the countryside.

Retracing her route and hunting down leads on the blue pickup, she unravels the giant's identity: Michael Anderson. But the real shock comes with the family connection: the sweet library director who gave her the shortcut, Ramona Norville, *was his mother*. The matriarca had been routinely sending solitary women down Stagg Road to feed her son's depravity.

> *I genuinely did not see that coming. I honestly assumed the librarian gave the tip in good faith and Tess just had terrible luck. That reveal blindsided me completely.*

Armed with her .38, Tess tracks Michael to his auto repair garage. In cold, methodical retribution, she corners the giant and shoots him dead, avenging herself and the forgotten women in the culvert.

Refusing to leave loose ends that could claim future victims, Tess finishes the job: she confronts and eliminates the complicit mother as well. She closes the circle of violence in absolute silence, vanishing into the night and slipping back into her ordinary life without a trace.

> *That conclusion was incredibly satisfying. It balanced high tension with crisp pacing, reminding me a lot of the movie I Spit on Your Grave. A fantastic, gripping novella.*

## Fair Extension

> *Riding high off the first two stories, my expectations were through the roof for this third one. Realistically, not every piece in a collection can hit the exact same high gear. Fair Extension felt decidedly "ok" and functioned mainly as a breather. That proved especially true once I hit the heavy hitter that wraps up the book.*

The third novella introduces Dave Streeter, living in King's iconic town of Derry, Maine. He receives a grim medical death sentence: aggressive, metastatic cancer in his lungs and liver, leaving him with only a handful of agonizing months.

Driving dazed along the shoulder of Route 15, Streeter spots an unusual roadside stand with a simple sign reading: "Extensions". Behind the counter stands a sharp-dressed figure with pointed teeth and unnerving eyes calling himself Mr. Elvid — a transparent anagram for *Devil*.

Elvid peddles extensions of all kinds: credit limits, hair, patience... but for Dave, he offers a life extension: fifteen healthy, vigorous years, with the option to renew for another fifteen when the term expires.

> *I liked this opening because it took a different angle from the first two. The setup is immediate, revolving around a classic Faustian pact, but it reels you into the dilemma effortlessly.*

The deal doesn't demand vast sums of money; just a small percentage of Streeter's annual income. The true cost is purely ethical: for Dave to enjoy renewed health, all his accumulated cancer, agony, and misfortune must be dumped onto someone else. And the rule is non-negotiable: *it must be someone Dave genuinely, venomously loathes*.

Without batting an eye, Streeter names Tom Goodhugh, his closest lifelong friend and business partner. The catch is that beneath decades of loyal camaraderie, Dave secretly nurtured bitter, toxic envy toward Tom: Tom was taller, handsomer, struck it rich with a thriving sanitation company, and enjoyed an idyllic marriage with brilliant kids.

The bargain is struck and the results are instant. Days later, Streeter's oncologist is left speechless: the tumors vanished without a trace. Dave feels better than ever, his investments soar, and his kids land sudden prestigious career wins.

While Streeter prospers, Tom Goodhugh is dragged through a modern-day Book of Job:

- Tom's wife is diagnosed with virulent breast cancer and dies after an agonizing, degrading decline.
- His oldest son, a golden-boy athlete with a bright future, has his reputation ruined in a cheating scandal and is shortly thereafter paralyzed from the neck down in an accident.
- His daughter gives birth to a stillborn, deformed child and spirals into crippling clinical depression.
- Tom's acclaimed waste-management business crumbles under environmental violations and embezzlement scandals, wiping him out financially and driving him to severe alcoholism.

> *The most chilling aspect of this story is watching Streeter witness his best friend's systematic destruction without a shred of guilt. He watches Tom's misery with quiet, smug satisfaction. There’s no divine retribution or moral redemption here: evil wins cleanly, and Dave lives happily ever after at the expense of another man's life.*

## A Good Marriage

> *Coming off the shorter, lighter third story, I anticipated another quick, predictable wrap-up. In my head, a balanced four-story collection usually has two bangers and two average fillers. But this last novella delivered a massive, unexpected gut punch.*

Closing out the collection in style, the final novella centers on Darcy and Bob Anderson, enjoying an enviable suburban life outside Portland, Maine. Married for 27 comfortable years with two successful adult children, their quiet, affectionate partnership is the picture of domestic stability. Bob is a methodical accountant who loves collecting rare coins.

Everything shatters on an ordinary weekend while Bob is out of town attending a numismatic convention.

On Saturday night, Darcy slips down into the dark garage hunting for spare batteries for the TV remote. Rummaging near her husband's worktable, her shoe knocks against a cardboard box concealed beneath a loose piece of plywood.

Inside, she initially discovers extreme sadomasochistic pornography. But the real horror awaits inside a small ID holder: the driver's license and personal cards of Marjorie Duvall — a woman brutally raped, tortured, and murdered nearby just days earlier.

Cross-referencing Bob's business trips with unsolved murders across New England, Darcy faces the most terrifying truth imaginable: the calm, gentle man she shared a bed and family with for nearly three decades is "Beadie", the notorious serial killer who spent years hunting, mutilating, and murdering women.

> *This was a phenomenal twist. The horror of discovering that your lifelong soulmate is actually an unrepentant monster was executed brilliantly. Darcy’s internal spiral — the disbelief, the denial, the sheer vertigo of "How could I not have known?" — felt devastatingly realistic. It makes you shudder wondering if scenarios like this happen in real life.*

Darcy tries to mask her panic over the phone, but her unsteady voice tips Bob off, and he races home early. Finding the loose floorboard disturbed, a suffocating, silent standoff begins in the kitchen.

Bob doesn't bother denying it. He confesses to harboring a second persona named "Beadie" (named after a childhood friend), but swears to Darcy that now that the truth is out, he'll never kill again. Then comes the emotional blackmail: if Darcy goes to the authorities, the fallout will ruin both their children's lives, derail their daughter's upcoming wedding, and taint the family with eternal public shame.

> *That psychological chess game is brilliant and painfully believable. Bob's manipulation tactics are twisted variations of everyday gaslighting, showing just how calculating an abusive or psychopathic mind can be.*

Darcy feigns agreement to buy time, but their home becomes a claustrophobic cage. Lying in bed next to a monster every night drains her will to live; she lives in terror of being strangled in her sleep, seeing in Bob's cold eyes that he’s merely waiting for things to quiet down before staging a fatal "accident" for her.

Recognizing that going to the police would ruin her kids and staying with Bob meant certain death, Darcy realizes her only path forward is to kill him herself and make it look like a domestic mishap.

Waiting for an opportune night after encouraging him to drink heavily, she sets her trap at the top of the stairs. As Bob heads up, Darcy shoves him violently down the wooden steps. With Bob battered and wheezing on the landing, she descends and methodically suffocates him with plastic bags and dish towels until he stops breathing.

Forensics rule the death a tragic, alcohol-fueled fall down the stairs. Bob is laid to rest with full community honors as a beloved citizen, leaving Darcy alone with her terrible secret. Months later, a retired, terminally ill investigator pieces together the truth, but recognizing Darcy's desperate, heroic dilemma, chooses to let the matter rest and take his suspicions to the grave.

> *This story surprised me with its psychological richness. The calculated tension, the moral ambiguity, and the resolve required to commit a justifiable murder to protect loved ones close out the collection masterfully.*

---

This book is an absolute home run. Going in, I anticipated a mild, middle-of-the-road read, and ended up with three unforgettable, razor-sharp stories that permanently raised my appreciation for the genre. They were grueling reads, but they proved beyond doubt that I can thoroughly love horror when driven by rich character psychology. A wholehearted recommendation: a definitive 5 out of 5.
