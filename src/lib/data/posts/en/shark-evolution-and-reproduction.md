## Survivors of five global apocalypses

Put a quick timeline in your head for a second. Roughly 240 million years ago, the first dinosaurs started wandering around. Around 380 million years ago, the very first true trees showed up on dry land. And those incredible icy rings orbiting Saturn? Astronomers estimate they're somewhere between 10 and 100 million years old.

Now wrap your head around this: **sharks were already cruising the oceans over 450 million years ago**.

Yep, you read that right. Shark ancestors are older than trees, older than dinosaurs, and older than the rings of Saturn.

They’ve survived all **five major mass extinction events** in Earth’s history — including the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs, and the Permian Great Dying that erased nearly 95% of all marine life from the planet.

How does a creature stick around that long without getting wiped off the map? The answer lies in ultra-durable biology and a way of having pups that looks totally different from most other fish.

---

## Why do natural history museums only have fossil teeth?

If sharks are so ancient, why don't we see fully mounted skeletons of them in museums the way we see T-Rex and Triceratops?

Blame the cartilage we talked about in that first anatomy post:

When a shark dies, its soft cartilage skeleton breaks down super fast in seawater before ocean sediments can turn it into stone. The only parts that survive intact are the teeth and the hard enamel-coated dermal scales on the skin.

Because every shark sheds and replaces tens of thousands of teeth over its lifetime, the ocean floor basically turned into a massive natural warehouse of fossilized teeth.

> *Of all those fossils, the Megalodon is easily the most famous: a prehistoric monster that lived a few million years ago, measured between 15 and 18 meters long, and sported teeth bigger than an adult human hand, built specifically to hunt ancient whales.*

## The reproduction game: quality over quantity

Most regular fish play the lottery game: a female broadcasts a million tiny eggs into the water, the male fertilizes the cloud, and both swim away. Almost all of them get eaten as plankton within minutes, but through sheer numbers, a lucky few make it to adulthood.

Sharks play the mammal game:

- They take years (or decades) to mature and have their first pups;
- They have very small litters (ranging from 2 to a few dozen);
- Pups are born large, strong, and ready to hunt for themselves from second zero.

> [!NOTE]
> **The patience champion:**
> Lemon sharks take 13 to 15 years to mature. Great whites only start breeding around 25 to 30 years old. But the all-time record across the entire animal kingdom belongs to the **Greenland shark**: it cruises through freezing Arctic waters for **over 400 years** and only reaches breeding age around **150 years old**!

> *A hundred and fifty years just to hit puberty. While human empires rose and fell and entire technologies came and went, a Greenland shark is still just getting ready for its first date in the dark Arctic ice.*

## Rough courtship and armored hides

Unlike regular fish that squirt eggs out into the water, shark reproduction is internal, with actual physical mating.

Males have two cartilaginous organs near their back fins called claspers, used to pass sperm to the female.

And since they don't have hands or limbs to hold on against ocean currents, the courtship ritual is pretty rough:

- The male chases the female down and bites hard onto her fins or back to lock her in place;
- It's very common to see adult females covered in healed bite scars across their backs;
- But evolution had a plan: female skin in many species is up to **three times thicker** than male skin, acting like a leather armor jacket to handle the beating.

## Virgin births in the deep

In 2001 at a US zoo, a female hammerhead gave birth to a healthy pup in a tank containing exclusively adult females that hadn’t seen a male in over three years.

They ran DNA tests on the pup and the results were mind-blowing: zero male DNA. The mother had produced the pup completely on her own through a process called parthenogenesis.

Similar cases have happened with zebra sharks and other species. Biologists think of this as nature's emergency button: if a female gets stranded in an area where males have been completely wiped out, she can still reproduce on her own to keep the lineage alive.

## Three different ways to enter the world

Depending on the species, shark birth takes three completely different routes:

### 1. Laying leathery eggs (mermaid's purses)
Some species lay eggs inside tough, leathery keratin cases equipped with spiral tendrils that anchor around rocks or kelp so the surge won't sweep them away. Once the pup breaks out, the empty pouch washes ashore — popularly known as "mermaid's purses".

### 2. True placenta and umbilical cord
Species like blue sharks and hammerheads carry their young almost exactly like mammals: the embryo connects to a real placenta with an umbilical cord inside the mother and absorbs nutrients directly from her bloodstream until giving birth to live young.

### 3. Sibling rivalry inside the womb
Most species hatch their eggs inside the mother's body and give birth to live pups. But in the sand tiger shark, things get taken to a wild level:

The very first pup to hatch inside the uterus grows sharp teeth before anyone else and **actively hunts down and devours all its smaller brothers and sisters right inside the mother's womb**. By the end of pregnancy, only a single surviving champion remains in each side of the uterus. When it’s finally born into the sea, it already measures nearly a meter long and is an experienced predator before ever seeing the light of day.

> *You barely open your eyes to the world and your very first task in life is fighting a brutal Battle Royale against your own siblings inside the womb. Stephen King would definitely get nightmares from this.*

## A blueprint that worked almost too well

This strategy of living a long time, having very few pups, and investing heavily into each one guaranteed the sharks' dominance for hundreds of millions of years. Nothing in nature could knock down that strategy.

The catch? It only works when humans aren't pulling millions of them out of the water every single year. An animal that takes decades to have its first offspring simply can't out-reproduce modern industrial fishing fleets.
