## Way beyond the "mindless killing machine"

If you ask pretty much anyone what goes through a shark’s head all day, the default answer is always the same: *"swim aimlessly, smell blood, and chomp anything that twitches"*. Suspense movies have done an incredible job painting sharks as brainless robots driven purely by the urge to tear things apart.

Spend some time reading about how they actually live in the wild, though, and reality is much cooler.

Sharks have clear social hierarchies, learn multi-step tasks quickly, use obvious body language to avoid useless brawls, play together in groups, and even team up when hunting.

Let's bust the biggest myths about how these guys actually behave.

---

## The solitary hunter myth

It’s true that many sharks spend long stretches cruising alone in the open sea. But the idea that they hate company is totally false:

- **Hammerhead conventions:** in places like Galápagos and the Gulf of California, hundreds of hammerheads gather every single day around underwater seamounts. It's not chaos: they have a defined social ladder, where larger females swim in the center and set the pace and direction for the entire crew;
- **Lunch etiquette:** when multiple sharks of different species stumble upon a whale carcass in the open sea, they don't immediately start tearing each other up. There's a clear code of respect based on size and aggression. Smaller sharks yield right of way to bigger ones, everyone gets to eat, and nobody gets injured for free;
- **Reef hunting packs:** whitetip reef sharks hunt in organized packs at night. They work coral crevices together: while some jam their snouts into holes to flush hiding fish out, others stand guard at the exits to catch whoever tries to bolt.

---

## Body language: the warning before the bite

Unlike movies where a shark teleports out of nowhere to chomp somebody, in real life they almost always warn you when they're stressed or annoyed.

If a diver corners a shark in a cave or crowds its personal bubble, it flashes very clear body signals:

- => Its back arches up into a noticeable hunch;
- => Its pectoral fins drop down stiffly;
- => Its swimming turns rigid, looping in jerky figure-eight curves.

It’s the underwater equivalent of a dog growling and showing its teeth. If you slowly back off, it calms down and swims away. A bite almost always only happens when someone insists on ignoring those blaring warnings.

---

## The dolphin beef: myth vs. reality

Who hasn't heard the story that *"sharks are terrified of dolphins and dolphins protect humans"*. It's a classic, but there’s a lot of confusion mixed into it.

Here’s the real deal:

### Do sharks hunt dolphins?
Yes, absolutely. Tiger sharks and great whites regularly target young, elderly, or stray dolphins. It’s common to spot adult dolphins sporting healed shark bite scars along their flanks.

### So why do sharks avoid pods of dolphins?
Because dolphins live in tight, organized groups and pack a brutal natural weapon: their **rock-hard bony snout** (*beak*).

When a shark tries to grab a dolphin calf, adult dolphins band together and charge like battering rams at full speed, slamming straight into the shark's gills and soft belly. Those impacts cause severe internal bleeding. No solitary hunter wants to risk taking a beating from five or ten angry dolphins at once.

### The actual nightmare: Orcas
If there’s one animal in the ocean that great white sharks genuinely dread, it’s **orcas** (which, biologically speaking, are the biggest dolphins in the world).

> [!NOTE]
> **The liver-hunting orcas:**
> In South Africa, researchers documented orcas hunting great whites with terrifying intelligence. They ram the shark and flip it upside down — which locks the shark into a reflex paralysis called tonic immobility. With the shark completely immobilized, the orca rips open the side of the body and surgically eats **only the liver**, letting the rest of the carcass sink. The mere presence of these orcas causes entire populations of great whites to abandon bays for months at a time.

## Speed: leisurely cruise vs. the Mako missile

Most of the time, sharks look downright lazy. They cruise along at a relaxed 2 to 8 km/h, because swimming all day burns precious fuel and nobody wants to waste calories for nothing.

But when it's time to accelerate, they turn into torpedoes:

The **shortfin mako** is widely considered the fastest of all, rocketing past **70 km/h** and leaping up to 9 meters clean out of the water while running down fast tuna.

How does it pull that off being a cold-blooded fish?

Truth is, it’s not completely cold-blooded. Makos and great whites have a specialized network of blood vessels in the center of their body that captures heat generated by their swimming muscles. This keeps their core muscles, eyes, and brain up to **7°C to 10°C warmer than the surrounding cold sea**. Warm muscle contracts way faster, delivering insane horsepower during an ambush.

---

## Intelligence and play

Assuming fish don't think is just human prejudice. Studies have proven that many shark species pick up routines quickly, solve puzzle tests for food rewards, and remember what they learned almost a year later without any refresher practice in between.

And what I find even cooler is that they also **play**:

Marine biologists have repeatedly filmed porbeagle sharks picking up floating pieces of kelp in their mouths, letting them drift, chasing them down again, and passing the seaweed back and forth with buddies, just like dogs playing fetch on the beach. No food involved, no aggression, just pure play.

---

## How do sharks actually sleep?

If most of them have to keep moving to avoid sinking and to breathe, how do they ever rest?

It depends on how each species breathes:

1. **Those that pump water:** bottom-dwelling species (like nurse sharks) simply lie down on the sand or in rocky caves and sleep with their eyes open for hours, keeping water rhythmically pumping through their gills without moving an inch;
2. **Those that swim nonstop:** species that must stay in constant motion (like blue sharks) have their swimming rhythm wired directly into their **spinal cord**, not their main brain. They can essentially power down parts of their conscious brain while their spinal cord keeps their tail beating on cruise control.

---

## Bizarre hunting styles

To wrap up, not every shark hunts with a straightforward torpedo charge:

- **The thresher shark:** has a massive tail fin almost as long as the rest of its entire body. It rounds up schools of fish and uses its tail like a bullwhip cracking over 80 km/h through the water, stunning dozens of fish at once with the acoustic shockwave before casually circling back to eat;
- **The carpet shark (wobbegong):** lies flat on rocky reefs, covered in skin tassels that look exactly like seaweed. When an unsuspecting octopus or crab strolls past, it opens its mouth so fast it creates an underwater vacuum, inhaling prey whole in the blink of an eye.

As you can see, the daily life of these creatures is way more complex and fascinating than horror movies ever let on.
