## Over 500 completely different faces

When someone mentions the word "shark", almost everyone pictures the exact same mental movie: a triangular grey dorsal fin cutting through sunny beach waters and a mouth full of razor-sharp teeth. Basically, everybody imagines a movie great white or a tiger shark.

That archetype is just a tiny sliver of the iceberg.

Today, science recognizes **more than 500 living shark species** scattered across every ocean on the planet — from 8-inch deep-sea creatures glowing neon blue in the pitch black to gentle giants bigger than a school bus.

To organize this massive family, marine biologists divide all modern sharks into **eight main groups (known as orders)**.

Let's take a look at each of them without getting bogged down in boring academic jargon.

---

## 1. Hexanchiformes: the ancient six- and seven-gilled crew

If you want to know what a shark looked like when the very first dinosaurs were roaming around, look at this group. They are the most primitive, ancient sharks still swimming out there today.

- **The trademark:** almost every modern shark has exactly 5 pairs of gill slits on its neck. This crew still has **6 or 7 gill slits** on each side;
- **A single fin:** they only have one dorsal fin on their back (most sharks have two), tucked way back near their tail;
- **Who's in the club:** the bluntnose sixgill shark (a deep-water beast reaching 5 meters) and the bizarre **frilled shark**, which has a body like a prehistoric sea snake and rows of needle-sharp trident teeth.

> *The frilled shark looks like a living fossil that simply forgot to go extinct. If you ran into one of these things in the dark, there's no way you wouldn't think you're staring at a mythological sea serpent.*

---

## 2. Squaliformes: spines and living light in the deep

With more than 125 species, this is the second-largest shark family in the world, ruling cold waters and ocean trenches.

- **The trademark:** they have no anal fin (that small belly fin near the tail), and most species pack sharp defensive spines in front of both back fins;
- **Glowing sharks:** many spend their lives in the pitch black of the deep sea and have tiny light-emitting pores on their skin that glow pale blue, helping erase their dark silhouette against surface light;
- **Who's in the club:** the spiny dogfish, the cookiecutter shark (which bites neat, circular melon-ball chunks out of larger fish and subs), deep-sea species found down past 3,000 meters, and the legendary **Greenland shark**, which can live over 400 years in freezing Arctic waters.

---

## 3. Pristiophoriformes: the true sawsharks

A small, quirky group that looks pretty wild:

- **The trademark:** their snout stretches out into a long, flat blade lined with sharp teeth down both sides, used like a hedge trimmer to stun schooling fish and dig through sand;
- **The two whiskers:** right in the middle of the saw, they sport two long, sensitive barbels used to feel for hidden prey in the seabed.

> [!NOTE]
> **A sawshark is not a sawfish!**
> The famous sawfish is actually a ray: its gill slits are located underneath on its belly, and it can grow up to 7 meters long. A sawshark is much smaller (rarely passing 1.5 meters) and has its gills on the sides of its neck, just like any proper shark.

---

## 4. Squatiniformes: the camouflaged angel sharks

At first glance, you’d look at one of these and swear up and down it’s a ray.

- **The trademark:** their body is completely flattened and their front pectoral fins are wide and wing-like, resembling angel wings. They spend their days buried in the sand waiting for a crab or flatfish to stroll past;
- **Why are they sharks?** Because their front fins aren’t fused to their head like rays, and their gill slits open on the sides of their body;
- **A sad reality:** because they lie right in shallow coastal sand, commercial bottom-trawling nets hit them hard, making them one of the most endangered shark families on Earth today.

---

## 5. Heterodontiformes: horn crests and shell-crushing teeth

A small group found mainly in the Pacific, like the Port Jackson shark and the horn shark.

- **The trademark:** a blunt, boxy head with two hard ridges above their eyes that look like miniature horns;
- **Two-in-one teeth:** sharp little teeth in the front to grab prey, and flat pavement-like molars in the back to grind down tough clams, crabs, and sea urchins;
- **Corkscrew eggs:** females lay tough egg cases shaped like spiral drill bits. The mother picks the newly laid egg up in her mouth and twists it into rock crevices so waves can’t sweep it away.

> *A mother shark using her own mouth to screw a spiral-threaded egg case into a rock crevice is the kind of thing that makes you go: how on earth did evolution invent this? Incredible stuff.*

---

## 6. Orectolobiformes: from living rugs to the ocean's giant

This group is wildly diverse, mixing opposites you’d never expect together:

- **The trademark:** a short mouth sitting right in front of their eyes, with little sensory whiskers near their nostrils;
- **The famous lineup:**
  - **The carpet shark (Wobbegong):** a flattened body covered in fringed skin flaps that look like kelp, making it an invisible ambush ninja on coral reefs;
  - **The nurse shark:** the calm, friendly bottom-dweller we often see resting in aquarium caves with its friends;
  - **The whale shark:** the undisputed biggest fish on the planet, cruising past 18 meters long while peacefully filtering tiny plankton without bothering a soul.

---

## 7. Lamniformes: the high-speed torpedoes

If you’re looking for raw speed and power, this is where you go. This group brings together some of the most famous open-ocean hunters in the world:

- **The trademark:** muscular, perfectly streamlined torpedo bodies built for high-speed cruising, almost all equipped with that internal heating system that keeps muscles warmer than the cold ocean around them;
- **The dream team:**
  - The iconic **great white shark**;
  - The turbocharged **shortfin mako**, capable of crossing 70 km/h;
  - The **thresher shark**, which snaps its extra-long tail like a bullwhip;
  - The massive, gentle **basking shark**, filtering plankton along the surface;
  - The weird **goblin shark**, living in the abyss and shooting its jaws outward to snatch deep-sea squid;
  - And the extinct prehistoric titan, the **Megalodon**.

> *If you've never watched a video of a goblin shark slingshotting its jaw forward during a strike, do yourself a favor and look it up. It genuinely looks like a deleted scene from Ridley Scott's Alien.*

---

## 8. Carcharhiniformes: the rulers of the sea

Here sits the heavyweight champion in terms of species count. **More than half of all shark species on the planet** belong to this single order (nearly 300 different species!).

- **The trademark:** two dorsal fins without defensive spines, an anal fin on the belly, and that classic white third eyelid that flips shut to protect their eyes during a bite;
- **The household names:**
  - The mighty **tiger shark**, with its tiger-like dark stripes;
  - The **bull shark**, famous for being able to thrive in freshwater and swim hundreds of miles up rivers;
  - The sleek **blue shark**, wandering entire ocean basins;
  - The bold **oceanic whitetip**, ruler of the open blue;
  - And all species of **hammerheads**.

---

## Way more than a single silhouette

Reducing over 500 wildly different species to the caricature of a generic movie killer misses all the coolest parts of biology. There are tiny sharks that glow in the dark, sharks disguised as kelp rugs, massive gentle giants that eat microscopic shrimp, and sharks that lay corkscrew eggs.
