## The monster we invented

Close your eyes for three seconds and hum that iconic two-note movie score: *Dun-dun... Dun-dun... Dun-dun-dun-dun...*

It is virtually impossible to hear that bassline without picturing a monstrous shark stalking an unsuspecting beachgoer in shallow surf. Ever since the movie *Jaws* hit theaters in 1975, humanity bought into a collective panic: we started treating sharks like calculating suspense-movie villains obsessed with human flesh.

Step away from the screen, turn off sensationalized cable news, and actually look at real biological data, though, and reality is completely backwards.

Sharks do not hunt humans. In reality, it’s humans who are clearing sharks out of the oceans at a pace nature simply cannot replace.

Let's lay out the real numbers to see just how blown out of proportion this fear really is.

---

## Fear completely detached from reality

The **International Shark Attack File (ISAF)** is the official scientific organization that investigates and catalogs every shark-human encounter globally, going back to the 1500s.

The very first thing they do is split cases into two distinct groups:

- **Provoked encounters:** when a person messes with the animal first — a fisherman trying to rip a hook out of a shark's mouth, a diver tugging a nurse shark’s tail for a quick selfie, or a spearfisher swimming with bleeding fish tied to their waist;
- **Unprovoked encounters:** when a shark bites someone in open water with zero prior human interference.

And how many unprovoked bites happen each year across the **entire planet Earth**?

On average, there are between **60 and 80 bites per year worldwide**. And fatalities average between **4 and 8 deaths per year**.

Now take a look at how that compares to everyday hazards:

| What kills more people each year? | Estimated global annual deaths |
|---|---|
| **Mosquito bites (malaria/dengue)** | ~700,000 people |
| **Traffic accidents** | ~1,300,000 people |
| **Drownings in oceans and rivers** | ~236,000 people |
| **Lightning strikes** | ~24,000 people |
| **Coconuts falling from trees** | ~150 people |
| **Shark attacks** | **~5 to 8 people** |

The mathematical odds of you ever being bitten by a shark in your lifetime sit at roughly **1 in 11.5 million**. The odds of dying from one are roughly **1 in 264 million**. You are literally far more likely to be struck by lightning twice or have your kitchen toaster catch fire than to get bitten by a shark at the beach.

> *Looking at these numbers and realizing you're thirty times more likely to get taken out by a falling coconut on the beach than by a shark bite is the ultimate proof of how thoroughly cinema warped our perception of risk.*

## Why do shark bites actually happen?

Out of more than 500 cataloged shark species, **over 95% have never been involved in a single human incident**. Most are under 3 feet long and dart away in a panic the second any swimmer splashes near them.

Almost all severe encounters involve just four large species: the great white, the tiger shark, the bull shark, and the oceanic whitetip.

And why do they bite?

Sharks don't have hands to feel strange floating objects. Their mouth is their primary sensory tool. In most incidents involving surfers, the shark looks up from below against the bright sun and mistakes the dark silhouette of the surfboard and paddling limbs for a seal, sea lion, or sea turtle.

It surfaces, delivers what biologists call an **investigative test bite**, and instantly realizes its mistake. Tasting fiberglass, neoprene, and lean, bony human meat (to a shark, we have virtually zero caloric value compared to a blubbery seal), it lets go and swims away. Fatalities, when they tragically occur, are almost always caused by rapid blood loss from that single initial bite, not because the animal wanted to eat someone.

## The "Jaws Effect" and the author's regret

Following the massive success of *Jaws* in the 1970s, collective hysteria swept coastlines. Thousands of amateur fishermen headed out into the ocean to hunt and slaughter any shark they could find, ripping out the jaws to hang on garage walls as trophies of courage.

Shark populations cratered around the world.

> [!NOTE]
> **Peter Benchley's lifelong regret:**
> The author of the book that inspired the movie was so horrified by the real-life slaughter his fictional story caused that he spent the final 30 years of his life working as one of the world's most passionate shark defenders. He publicly stated: *"Knowing what I know now, I could never have written that book today. Sharks don't target humans out of malice; they are the true victims in this story."*

> *Watching the creator of Jaws dedicate the rest of his life to undoing the cultural damage his own book caused is a punch to the gut. It really shows the staggering weight a well-told story can have on the real world.*

## The real massacre: 100 million dead sharks every year

While sharks kill between 5 and 8 humans per year, do you know how many sharks humans kill every single year?

Roughly **100 million sharks**.

That works out to more than **11,000 dead sharks every single hour**.

A huge portion of this slaughter stems from a brutal practice called **shark finning**:
- Fishermen catch a shark on a longline;
- Slice off all its fins with a hot blade;
- Toss the animal back into the sea while it’s still alive, because its body takes up too much room on the boat and isn't worth as much money;
- Without fins, the shark sinks like a rock to the ocean floor and slowly suffocates or gets eaten alive by scavengers.

And all of that just to supply the market for traditional **shark fin soup** in Asia, served as a luxury status symbol at lavish banquets. The most absurd part? Fin cartilage **tastes like absolutely nothing**. It only provides a gelatinous noodle texture to the soup, which has to be heavily seasoned with chicken or pork broth to have any flavor at all.

> *Slaughtering 100 million apex predators every year just to provide a flavorless, jelly-like texture for party soup is the kind of sickening futility that turns your stomach.*

## You eat shark and probably don't even know it

Around the world, a sneaky level of marketing disguises shark meat in restaurants and grocery stores:

In Brazil, it’s labeled as **"cação"**. In Australia and the UK, it often masquerades in fish and chips shops as **"flake"** or **"rock salmon"**.

**Cação and flake are shark.** There's no separate fish in biology called "cação"; it's just a friendly commercial brand name used to sell shark and ray meat without scaring off customers.

And there's a genuine health issue here:

Because sharks sit at the top of the marine food chain and live for decades, they accumulate high levels of heavy industrial toxins over their lifetime, especially **methylmercury**. Health agencies around the globe warn that pregnant women and young children should strictly avoid eating shark meat, due to the risk of mercury interfering with brain development.

---

## What happens if sharks disappear?

Sharks haven’t ruled the oceans for over 450 million years by accident. They are essential to keeping the entire marine world functioning:

1. **They keep mid-level predators in check:** when you remove sharks from an ecosystem, mid-sized predatory fish multiply unchecked and devour all the herbivorous fish;
2. **They save coral reefs:** without herbivorous fish to graze on algae, thick seaweed overgrows the reef framework, choking living corals until entire reefs die;
3. **They act as ocean sanitation:** by preying mostly on sick, weak, or dead animals, sharks stop bacterial outbreaks and infectious diseases from sweeping through healthy schools of fish.

Sharks don't need our love, but they urgently need our **respect and awareness**.
